## How to Use This Kit (Read This First)

This is not a template you fill in blanks. It is a method.

Healthcare leaders do not hire the person with the longest resume. They hire the person who can prove, in one page, that they can **protect clinical throughput, grow revenue, and fix a bottleneck with a number rather than a hunch.** Every section that follows exists to turn your work history into that proof.

Work through it in order:

1. Read Part 1 — internalize the 3-part metric formula. This is the engine.
2. Read Part 2 — build your resume in the click-ready template, section by section.
3. Pick the role set closest to your target from Part 3 — borrow the skeleton and the benchmarks, then swap in your real numbers.
4. Part 4 — adapt the cover letter template and study both worked examples.
5. Part 5 — run your finished draft through the 10 most common mistakes and fix every one you find.

If a number is not a *real* number from your own work, do **not** invent it. Every benchmark in this kit tells you the *target* — your job in the interview is to move your real department toward or past that target. The resume states what you actually did. When you don't yet have hard numbers, Part 1 shows you how to go get them.

# PART 1 — The 3-Part Metric Formula

Every single bullet in your experience section must answer one question: **"Show me the result, measured."** The formula is:

## Verb + Action + Quantified Result

Put another way:

> **[Executive Action Verb]** + **[Specific Operational Context]** + **[A Number That Proves It]**

### The Three Pieces, Dissected

**1. The Verb (never the word "Responsible for").**
Your verb must signal *change* or *control*, not attendance. Weak verbs — *assisted, handled, helped, worked with, oversaw, participated in* — describe someone who shows up. Executive verbs — *restructured, standardized, recovered, eliminated, negotiated, reduced, championed, redesigned, implemented* — describe someone who *produces results*. Replace the first list with the second.

**2. The Action (the specific operational context).**
This is what you actually did to the operation — the workflow you rebuilt, the team you led, the denial reason code you chased. No context, no credibility. "Reduced denials" tells a reviewer nothing. "Root-caused the top five denial reason codes (CO-4, CO-16, CO-197) and rebuilt the front-end eligibility scrubber" tells them you know exactly where the money was leaking.

**3. The Quantified Result (the number + the benchmark).**
This is the part 90% of applicants skip, and it is the entire point. Attach a specific number — a percentage, a day count, a dollar figure, an RVU, an FTE, a percentile — and, where possible, compare it to a benchmark so the reviewer can judge how good the number is.

### The Formula on One Line

> **Weak:** "Responsible for clinic scheduling and patient check-in."
> **Strong:** "Restructured provider appointment templates to cut third-next-available wait time from 24 days to 11 days, moving the clinic from the 25th to the 50th MGMA percentile."

### Can't Find Your Numbers Yet? Go Get Them — Here's How

Early-career candidates often tell me, "I don't have metrics." You do. You've just never looked at your work through a metric lens. Pull these from your last 12–24 months:

- **Pull the report your software already makes.** Your EMR and your practice-management system already track no-show rate, days in A/R, clean claim rate, denial rate, and how many encounters each provider completes per day. Ask your supervisor or your billing team for the last 12 months. It's a legitimate request and a skill-building one.
- **Dig through email and meeting decks.** Every quality meeting, every dashboard screenshot, every weekly operations email contains a number you touched.
- **If a number genuinely does not exist, measure the baseline once.** Run one month of data, make one improvement, run the next month, and report the delta. That is an entire bullet in 60 days.
- **Ask your question out loud in your current role:** "What do we track here, and where do we fall on it?" That single question gives you your benchmarks.

On your resume, use only numbers that are **true**. What you felt about your work matters less than what the numbers show you did.

# PART 2 — The Click-Ready Resume Template (with Section Guidance)

> **Formatting rules before the template:** One page if you have under 8 years of experience, two pages absolute max. 11pt body, 0.7–1.0" margins, a clean sans-serif font (Calibri, Arial, Helvetica). Save as PDF named `FirstName_LastName_Resume_HealthcareAdmin.pdf`. Do **not** use a photo, a table, columns, or color blocks — they break the ATS parser. One accent color for headers is fine.

## Section 1 — Header & Contact Line

```
[FIRST NAME] [LAST NAME], [MHA / MBA / MPH, if applicable]
[City, State] | [Phone] | [Email] | [linkedin.com/in/yourname]
```

**Guidance:** Use a professional email only (never `cutechick2020@`). Your LinkedIn URL should be custom, not the long string of numbers. The credentials after your name (MHA, CMPE, RHIA, FACHE if you hold them) tell the reviewer your level before they read a word. Master's-trained candidates: the degree goes here in the header, not hidden at the bottom.

## Section 2 — Professional Summary (3–4 lines, no "objective")

The summary is your answer to "who is this person and what do they do best?" — in **numbers, not adjectives**. It should name your setting, your scope, and your standout result. Do **not** write "Seeking a challenging position that utilizes my skills" — that is filler and it wastes your most valuable real estate.

```
Results-driven [ROLE] with [X] years leading [ambulatory / revenue cycle / patient access /
clinical operations] in [setting]. Known for [one signature achievement with a number],
improving [second metric] from [X] to [Y], and [third metric] while maintaining [quality
benchmark]. Master's-trained [MHA] with hands-on [certification]. [Optional: target org type
— e.g., "Seeking to bring metric-driven operations to a growing multi-site medical group."]
```

**Fill-in example:**
> Results-driven Practice Manager with 6 years leading multi-provider ambulatory operations. Known for cutting clinic no-show rates from 13.8% to 6.4% through conversational SMS workflows and building provider schedules that sustain 91% utilization — while lifting HCAHPS overall satisfaction to the 78th percentile. Master's-trained (MHA) and Certified Medical Practice Executive (CMPE).

## Section 3 — Core Competencies / Skills

Use **3 columns of 4–5 short phrases**, or a tight two-column list. Every skill must be a real tool or true capability you can defend in an interview — no buzzword stuffing. Include software by name (e.g., Epic, Cerner, NextGen, AthenaHealth, eClinicalWorks, Kareo, PointClickCare) and the specific functions you know.

```
**Operations & Access:** TNAA & access optimization | No-show reduction | LWBS management |
  Door-to-doctor throughput | Staffing grid & FTE variance control
**Revenue Cycle:** Clean claim rate >96% | Denial management (CO-4/CO-16/CO-197) | Days in A/R |
  POS collections | Cost-to-collect
**Quality & Compliance:** HCAHPS / Press Ganey | Joint Commission (TJC) & CMS survey readiness |
  PSI-90 & readmissions | HIPAA & coding compliance
**Systems:** Epic | Cerner | NextGen | PointClickCare | Microsoft Excel (pivot tables) | SQL basics
```

## Section 4 — Professional Experience (the engine of the resume)

Reverse-chronological: most recent first. Format for each role:

```
[ORG NAME] — [City, State]                                     [Month YYYY] – [Month YYYY]
[Exact Job Title]
• 4–6 bullets, each built on the 3-part formula
• Lead your strongest, most quantifiable achievement first
```

**Guidance:**
- **4–6 bullets** for your most recent role, 3–4 for older ones. Do not exceed 6.
- **Lead every role with your scope line:** the org size, the number of providers, the volume of encounters, the size of the budget you touched. This one line frames everything that follows.
- **Order bullets by business impact**, not by what you did most hours. Patient-access wins and revenue-cycle wins go at the top of the role.
- **Vary your verbs** — never start three bullets in a row with the same word.
- **Do not write "Responsible for [x]."** Write what the [x] **became**.
- A job title alone is not a bullet. "Managed front desk" appears on half of all resumes. "Redesigned front-desk registration queues to trim door-to-doctor time by 14 minutes" is a bullet.

**Quick scope-line templates:**
- Operated [X] ambulatory clinics supporting [X] physicians and [X] APPs, driving [X] annual patient encounters.
- Directed a [X]-person patient access / revenue cycle team with daily responsibility for [X] claims and $[X] monthly cash collections.
- Managed a [X]-bed [unit / skilled nursing facility] with [X] direct reports and a $[X] annual operating budget.

## Section 5 — Education & Certifications

```
[UNIVERSITY] — [City, State]                              [Year]
Master of Health Administration (MHA) / MBA-HA / MPH

[UNIVERSITY] — [City, State]                              [Year]
Bachelor of [Science in Nursing / Business / Health Administration]

**Certifications (dated):**
• Certified Medical Practice Executive (CMPE) — MGMA, [Year]
• Fellow, American College of Healthcare Executives (FACHE / ACHE Fellow), [Year]
• Registered Health Information Administrator (RHIA) / Certified Coding Specialist (CCS), [Year]
• Certified Professional Coder (CPC) — AAPC, [Year]
• Six Sigma Green Belt, [Year]
```

**Guidance:** Place education here at the bottom unless you are a new graduate with little experience — then move it above experience. For each certification, state the **issuing body** (MGMA, ACHE, AHIMA, AAPC) — that is what tells a reviewer the credential is real and rigorous. Do not list an expired certification without noting it. Do not list a certification you are not actively pursuing as if you hold it.

## Go / No-Go Checklist Before You Submit

- [ ] Summary contains at least two real numbers.
- [ ] Every experience bullet follows Verb + Action + Quantified Result.
- [ ] Every bullet contains a specific number (%, $, days, RVU, FTE, percentile).
- [ ] Scope line opens every role.
- [ ] No weak verbs ("responsible for," "assisted," "helped").
- [ ] One page (or two max), no photo, no tables, no columns.
- [ ] Saved as PDF, filename `FirstName_LastName_HealthcareAdmin.pdf`.
- [ ] Zero typos — read it aloud, backward, and have someone else read it.

# PART 3 — Eight Role-Specific Bullet Sets with Real Benchmarks

Use the set for your target role as your skeleton. The **benchmark** (MGMA percentile, industry target) is your reference point — state where the metric *started*, where you moved it *to*, and how that compares to the target. Swapping in your own true numbers is not only allowed, it is the assignment.

To state your benchmark on the resume cheaply, add a "vs. industry benchmark of X" tag or save the benchmark framing for the interview. Benchmarks are shown here in **bold** so you know the target.

## 1. Practice Manager

**Scope line:** "Led daily operations for a 6-provider primary care practice generating 34,000 annual patient encounters and $4.1M in net revenue."

- Redesigned provider scheduling templates to reduce **third-next-available (TNAA) appointment** wait time by 13 days (from 24 to 11 days) — against an **industry standard of <5–7 days for primary care**, taking the practice from roughly the 25th to the 50th **MGMA percentile**.
- Cut the clinic **no-show and late-cancellation rate** from 13.8% to 6.4% across 6 providers by deploying two-way conversational SMS reminders, recovering an estimated 960 lost appointment slots or ~$86,000 in annual revenue (**benchmark target: <6–8%**).
- Maintained 91% **provider schedule utilization** across 8 clinical sessions per week by building a dynamic waitlist backfill process (**benchmark target: 88–92%**).
- Reduced front-desk **door-to-doctor wait time** by 14 minutes through digital pre-registration and rooming-huddle checklists, supporting an overall **HCAHPS** patient satisfaction lift from the 52nd to the 78th percentile (**benchmark: top quartile**).
- Managed a $4.1M operating budget; trimmed **premium overtime** from 6.1% to 1.9% of productive labor hours (**benchmark ceiling: 2.0–2.5%**) by rebuilding the staffing grid to hold 1.5–2.0 MAs per provider (**MGMA benchmark**).

# PART 4 — Cover Letter Template + Two Worked Examples

## The Cover Letter Template

Keep it to **4 tight paragraphs on one page**. Recruiters skim — three things they hunt for: the role, the one number that proves you can do it, and that you actually read their organization. Structure:

```
[Your Name]
[City, State] | [Phone] | [Email] | [LinkedIn URL]

[Date]

[Hiring Manager Name, if known — otherwise "Hiring Manager"]
[Practice / Organization Name]
[Street Address, City, State ZIP]

Re: [Exact Job Title], [Job ID if listed]

Dear [Mr./Ms./Dr. Last Name],

[Para 1 — The hook: 1–2 sentences. Name the role, name your credential, and
lead with your single most relevant number. Do not say "I am excited to
apply." Say what you have already done.]

[Para 2 — The proof: 1–2 sentences. Give the one or two quantified
achievements that match THIS role's top priority. Mine the job description
for its #1 stated pain point and mirror it back with your result.]

[Para 3 — The fit: 2–3 sentences. Two or three short lines showing you
understand their setting, their patient population, or their scale. Reference
something specific about the organization — NOT a generic compliment.]

[Para 4 — The close: 1–2 sentences + sign-off. Restate value, invite the
next step, thank them. Sign with your name.]

Sincerely,

[Your Name]
[Phone] | [Email]
```

# PART 5 — The 10 Most Common Resume Mistakes (and the Fix)

Every one of these appears on real healthcare-administration resumes every week. Run your draft against this list before you submit anything.

**1. "Responsible for" openings.**
Duty lists that read like a job description prove you can be scheduled, not that you can perform. **Fix:** Convert every "Responsible for X" into Verb + Action + Quantified Result. "Responsible for clinic scheduling" → "Restructured appointment templates to cut TNAA wait time from 24 to 11 days."

**2. Zero numbers.**
No percentages, no days, no dollars, no RVUs, no FTEs, no percentiles. **Fix:** Every bullet gets a specific number. If you don't have the number, go get it (Part 1 shows how) or measure a one-month baseline.

**3. Perfectionism over clarity.**
Bullets so long they are sentences in a paragraph. **Fix:** One line per bullet, hard cap at two lines, front-loaded verb, number at the end. ATS and humans both reward scannability.

**4. A job title masquerading as an achievement.**
"Managed the front desk" or "Supervised the billing team" tells scale but not impact. **Fix:** Add scope plus outcome — "Directed a 9-person billing team processing 3,200 claims and $1.9M in monthly cash; raised clean claim rate to 97.2%."

**5. Buzzword stuffing.**
"Dynamic, results-driven, synergistic, best-in-class" — no reviewer can fact-check these, so no reviewer trusts them. **Fix:** Delete every adjective that carries no number, and let the metric do the selling.

**6. Typos and formatting that break the parser.**
Tables, columns, photos, text boxes, and inconsistent headings corrupt ATS extraction and look unprofessional. **Fix:** Clean single-column markdown-friendly layout, standard ## headings, and a typo pass done out loud and backward. Healthcare is a documentation profession — a typo on a resume signals carelessness with charts.

**7. Gaps left unexplained.**
An unexplained 8-month gap invites a negative assumption. **Fix:** Briefly account for real gaps on the timeline (e.g., "Graduate program — MHA, 2025–2026" or a sabbatical-career interval) so the reviewer reads facts, not fiction.

**8. Irrelevant work history eating the page.**
Six summer jobs from a decade ago crowd out the operations achievements that matter. **Fix:** Cut anything that does not move toward healthcare operations; keep only a 1-line mention of early work that shows transferable skill. One strong page beats two padded ones.

**9. Generic summary / objective paragraph.**
"Seeking a challenging position utilizing my strong communication skills" appears on thousands of resumes and differentiates no one. **Fix:** Replace with a 3–4 line summary carrying at least two real numbers and your setting + scope.

**10. Fudging or overclaiming numbers.**
Inventing "increased revenue by 300%" or claiming a certification you don't hold — healthcare hiring networks are small and the industry runs on trust and compliance. A fabricated metric is a career-ending discovery, not a resume risk. **Fix:** State only numbers you can defend, source, and reproduce in an interview. If a number isn't yours, don't put your name on it.
