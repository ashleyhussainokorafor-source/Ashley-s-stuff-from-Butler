# 30 Operational & Metrics-Driven Interview Questions with STAR Answer Scripts

### Q1. Our primary care clinics have run a no-show rate near 14% for two years. How would you fix it, and what would you expect to move?
**Why they ask it:** It tests whether you treat access as a systems problem with measurable leakage rather than blaming patients.
**What strong candidates do:** Diagnose the driver — lead time, visit type, payer, transportation — and name a specific intervention sequence tied to a target rate.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** As clinic manager for a six-provider family medicine practice with about 10,000 annual visits, I inherited a 13.8% no-show and late-cancellation rate — roughly 1,380 lost encounters a year — while average lead time to a new appointment was 21 days.
- **Task:** I owned the access metrics and was asked to cut no-show leakage by a third within two quarters without adding provider hours.
- **Action:**
  - Stratified twelve months of cancellation data by lead time, visit type, and payer, and found appointments booked more than 14 days out drove 62% of no-shows while same-week bookings ran at 5.1%.
  - Launched two-way conversational SMS reminders with self-service rescheduling at 7 days, 48 hours, and 3 hours, plus a live waitlist that auto-backfilled released slots within 15 minutes.
  - Shortened follow-up intervals so established patients were booked 7 to 10 days out instead of 21, and required a $25 no-show deposit for recurring no-show patients on self-pay and high-deductible plans.
- **Result:** The no-show rate fell from 13.8% to 6.4% in five months — inside the under-6-to-8% target band — recovering roughly 740 encounters and about $96,000 in annualized net revenue, while schedule utilization held at 91%.
**Metrics to name:** No-show and late-cancellation rate (target under 6–8%, red flag over 12%); third-next-available appointment (primary care under 5–7 days); schedule utilization (88–92%); lead time to appointment; recovered encounter volume.
**Trap to avoid:** Saying "we sent reminders" with no baseline number, no target, and no segmentation explaining why reminders actually worked.

### Q2. A specialty clinic's no-show rate for new patient consults is 18%, and referring physicians are starting to send patients elsewhere. What do you do?
**Why they ask it:** New-patient no-shows are the most expensive kind — they waste a consult slot and damage the referral relationships that feed the practice.
**What strong candidates do:** Separate new-patient no-shows from established ones, protect the consult template, and address the real barrier — wait time, cost transparency, and referral friction.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** I managed an eight-provider cardiology practice where new-patient consult no-shows had climbed to 18.2% versus 7.4% for established follow-ups, and lead time for a new consult had stretched to 34 days. Referring primary care physicians were calling to ask where their patients had gone.
- **Task:** As practice administrator, I was accountable for new-patient access and referral volume, which had declined 11% year over year.
- **Action:**
  - Confirmed the diagnosis with data — new-patient no-shows tracked with consults booked beyond 21 days (a 22% no-show rate) and with patients who never received the intake packet before the visit.
  - Built a first-visit concierge workflow: a dedicated scheduler called every new referral within 24 hours, verified insurance and estimated out-of-pocket cost, sent a 90-second video explaining the first visit, and offered a same-week slot from a protected daily block.
  - Moved two of eight providers to a rolling two-week hold for new consults and put new-patient show rate on the weekly huddle board as a standing metric.
- **Result:** New-consult no-shows dropped from 18.2% to 8.1% in four months, consult lead time fell from 34 to 13 days, and referral volume recovered 9% while established follow-up no-shows held at 7.4%.
**Metrics to name:** New-patient versus established no-show rate; third-next-available appointment (specialty under 14 days); referral volume and leakage; consult template utilization; financial clearance / point-of-service collection rate.
**Trap to avoid:** Applying a one-size reminder fix to new patients without the cost transparency and lead-time work — those are the actual drivers of new-consult no-shows.

### Q3. You inherit a family medicine provider producing about 3,800 wRVUs a year, well below MGMA benchmarks. How do you approach it?
**Why they ask it:** It separates managers who understand wRVUs and MGMA percentiles from those who just tell people to work harder.
**What strong candidates do:** Name the benchmark, test the structural drivers first — MA ratio, template design, documentation lag — and bring the provider into the diagnosis with data rather than a punitive conversation.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** I took over an eight-physician primary care group where one family medicine physician was producing 3,800 wRVUs annually — below the MGMA 25th percentile of roughly 4,200 — against a group median of 4,900. The provider had a full panel and was working long hours, so volume wasn't the issue; throughput was.
- **Task:** As operations manager, my job was to tighten the group's productivity spread and bring that provider to at least the 40th percentile without burning them out.
- **Action:**
  - Ran a workflow time study and found the provider shared one medical assistant with 1.8 providers, against the MGMA-aligned 1.5 to 2.0 MA-per-provider model, and was spending 90 minutes a day on inbox and refill work after clinic.
  - Rebalanced support to give the provider a dedicated MA, moved refills and prior authorizations to a centralized clinical support pod, and shifted routine nurse-visit follow-ups to a pharmacist-run clinic.
  - Worked with the provider to convert two 20-minute slots per session to 15-minute slots for established, low-acuity visits using pre-visit chart scrubbing, and reviewed a monthly wRVU and encounters-per-session dashboard one-on-one.
- **Result:** That provider's production rose from 3,800 to 4,750 wRVUs over the next twelve months — just under the 50th percentile — while the group's wRVU per FTE moved from 4,950 to 5,180 with no increase in after-hours documentation time.
**Metrics to name:** wRVUs versus MGMA 25th/50th/75th percentile (family medicine roughly 4,200/4,900/5,800); MA-to-provider ratio (1.5–2.0); encounters per session; documentation lag and after-hours "pajama time"; template utilization.
**Trap to avoid:** Leading with discipline — the provider was already working hard; the real constraints were support ratio and template design.

### Q4. How would you raise a 20-provider multi-specialty group's productivity from the 30th percentile to the median in one fiscal year?
**Why they ask it:** It tests whether you can build a system-level productivity program rather than coach a single physician.
**What strong candidates do:** Distinguish structural barriers from behavioral ones, give a phased playbook, and defend the sequence with benchmarks.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** I was director of operations for a 20-provider multi-specialty group sitting at the 30th percentile for wRVUs per physician — roughly 8% below the MGMA median for our mix. Margin was negative and leadership wanted median productivity within a year.
- **Task:** I owned the productivity plan across primary care, orthopedics, and endocrinology, targeting the 50th percentile without lengthening patient wait times.
- **Action:**
  - Segmented all 20 providers by MGMA percentile and separated the six below the 25th percentile flagged as structural (support or template) from the three flagged as schedule or behavioral, then addressed the two groups differently.
  - Standardized appointment templates by visit type using billing and encounter data, freeing an average of 1.2 slots per session group-wide, and deployed a float MA pool to the five providers whose support ratio sat below 1.5.
  - Built a monthly productivity scorecard — wRVUs per FTE, wRVUs per session, panel size, and no-show rate — and reviewed it in a peer setting so each physician saw where they stood against their own specialty median, not just against a directive from me.
- **Result:** The group's wRVU per physician rose from the 30th to the 48th percentile in eleven months, primary care moved from 4,600 to 5,050 median wRVUs, and new-patient third-next-available improved from 19 to 12 days — so access improved even as volume grew.
**Metrics to name:** wRVUs per FTE and per session; MGMA percentile spread; panel size; template utilization; no-show rate; third-next-available appointment; contribution margin per provider.
**Trap to avoid:** Applying one standard productivity push to every provider — if you don't separate structural from behavioral causes you alienate the physicians who can't hit the target for reasons outside their control.

### Q5. Front-end authorization denials tripled after our EHR go-live. Walk me through how you'd find and fix the failure across scheduling, intake, and patient financial services.
**Why they ask it:** Denials are a cross-functional systems failure, and the answer reveals whether you can trace a metric back to its root cause.
**What strong candidates do:** Anchor on baseline and benchmark, trace denials by payer and reason code, and fix the top reasons before launching broad retraining.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Six months after an EHR conversion at a 40-provider multi-specialty group, our initial denial rate had jumped from 3.2% to 8.4% — more than double the under-4% top-performer benchmark — and net days in A/R had drifted from 34 to 47. The denials concentrated in missing prior authorizations and registration errors.
- **Task:** As revenue cycle manager, I owned denial rate and A/R, and the CFO wanted us back under 5% denials within two quarters.
- **Action:**
  - Pulled 90 days of denials by reason code and found CO-197 (missing precertification) and CO-16 (missing or incorrect information) made up 64% of new denials, with imaging and orthopedics the biggest offenders.
  - Rebuilt the authorization workflow so scheduling could not book a high-dollar procedure without a flagged auth task, added a real-time eligibility and benefits scrubber at pre-registration, and created a dedicated denial-prevention analyst role to work clearinghouse rejections before they aged.
  - Re-trained front desk and scheduling by department instead of one mass session, and stood up a weekly denial huddle with the top five reason codes posted until the trend broke.
- **Result:** Initial denial rate fell from 8.4% to 3.6% in five months, clean claim rate climbed from 88% to 96.5%, and net days in A/R dropped from 47 to 36 — recovering an estimated $1.1M in at-risk net revenue over two quarters.
**Metrics to name:** Initial denial rate (under 4% best, 5–10% average, over 12% red flag); clean claim rate (over 96–97% best); days in A/R (under 30 excellent, 30–45 acceptable); top denial reason codes (CO-197, CO-16, CO-4); first-pass resolution rate.
**Trap to avoid:** Jumping to "retrain everyone" before quantifying which reason codes and departments are actually driving the denials.

### Q6. Our net collection rate has slipped to 89% and nearly a quarter of our A/R is over 90 days. Where do you start?
**Why they ask it:** It tests whether you understand the difference between gross and net collections and can attack A/R aging rather than just "work harder."
**What strong candidates do:** Segment A/R by aging bucket and payer, quantify the collectible opportunity, and prioritize the oldest, largest balances with a defined follow-up cadence.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** I inherited the billing office for a 55-provider group where net collection rate had fallen to 89% — below the MGMA minimum of 95% — and 23% of A/R was over 90 days, with 11% over 120 days. Cash collections were running $380K behind budget year to date.
- **Task:** As director of revenue cycle, I was tasked with restoring net collections to at least 95% and cutting A/R over 90 days below 15% within two quarters.
- **Action:**
  - Ran an A/R aging and payer-mix analysis and found two commercial payers plus Medicare Advantage held 71% of the over-90-day balance, mostly claims that had never been followed up or had appeal deadlines about to expire.
  - Built a tiered work queue: every claim over 60 days routed to a dedicated high-dollar specialist, appeals filed within seven days of denial, and a weekly "old money" sprint forcing claims over 120 days to rebill, appeal, or documented write-off within 30 days.
  - Fixed the front-end cause by enforcing eligibility verification at scheduling, moving to daily charge capture reconciliation, and adopting a no-write-off-without-approval policy so we stopped masking process failures behind adjustments.
- **Result:** Net collection rate recovered from 89% to 95.8% in six months, A/R over 90 days fell from 23% to 14%, and days in A/R dropped from 48 to 37 — about $1.9M in accelerated cash on a $60M revenue base.
**Metrics to name:** Net (adjusted) collection rate (MGMA minimum 95%, HFMA 97–99%); days in A/R (under 30 excellent, 30–45 acceptable, over 45 red flag); percent A/R over 90 days (under 10% excellent, over 20% red flag); percent A/R over 120 days (under 5%); cost to collect (3–5%, best near 2%).
**Trap to avoid:** Managing to a gross collection rate or writing off old A/R to make the aging look clean — that hides the leak instead of fixing it.

### Q7. Our ED's door-to-doctor time is 47 minutes and patients are leaving without being seen at nearly 5%. What's your plan?
**Why they ask it:** LWBS and door-to-doctor are the clearest signals of a broken intake process, and the question tests throughput thinking.
**What strong candidates do:** Bench the numbers against national standards, separate intake delay from boarding, and sequence quick wins ahead of structural change.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** I was operations director for a 320-bed community hospital whose ED saw 58,000 visits a year. Door-to-doctor time was 47 minutes against a 15-to-20-minute target, left-without-being-seen was 4.8% versus a national median near 2%, and overall ED length of stay was 226 minutes.
- **Task:** I was accountable for ED throughput and given a 90-day mandate to cut LWBS by half and door-to-doctor below 30 minutes.
- **Action:**
  - Time-stamped the intake process and found the triage-to-provider gap was driven by a single-provider coverage model from 11am to 7pm, when arrival volume peaked 40% above the daily average.
  - Launched a split-flow model with a rapid medical evaluation zone staffed by an advanced practice provider at peak hours, moved registration to the bedside so it no longer blocked triage, and added a vertical-flow protocol for low-acuity patients who could be treated without ever occupying a bed.
  - Started daily bed huddles with inpatient nursing and case management to pull admitted ED patients upstairs faster, attacking boarding — which was consuming an average of 78 minutes of ED bed time per admission.
- **Result:** Door-to-doctor fell from 47 to 24 minutes in four months, LWBS dropped from 4.8% to 1.9% — below the national median — and overall ED length of stay improved from 226 to 188 minutes, while our ED patient experience top-box score rose six points.
**Metrics to name:** Door-to-doctor time (15–20 minutes); LWBS rate (national median about 2%, target under 2–3%); ED length of stay (national roughly 200–211 minutes); boarding time; triage-to-provider time; percent of ED visits admitted (about 19%).
**Trap to avoid:** Blaming volume alone — the data usually shows a coverage-to-arrival-pattern mismatch and boarding, not simply "too many patients."

### Q8. Half of our ED's delay is admitted patients boarding for beds. How do you fix throughput when the problem is upstream on the floors?
**Why they ask it:** It tests whether you understand that ED wait times are often a bed-management problem, not an ED problem.
**What strong candidates do:** Shift the lever to discharge timing, housekeeping turnaround, and bed assignment — and quantify the boarding hours recovered.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** At a 400-bed hospital, ED boarding averaged 112 minutes per admission and peaked around six hours on Mondays; occupancy ran 92% with a discharge peak at 4pm, so beds freed late couldn't absorb the morning admission wave.
- **Task:** As capacity management lead, I owned patient throughput across the ED, bed control, and inpatient units, with a goal of cutting boarding by a third.
- **Action:**
  - Mapped admission-to-bed times and found the real constraints were a 4pm discharge peak and a manual, phone-based bed assignment process adding roughly 40 minutes per bed.
  - Pushed discharge rounding earlier — set a discharge-before-noon goal, gave hospitalists a dedicated discharge pharmacist and transport so orders cleared by 1pm — and implemented electronic bed assignment with a discharge prediction tool.
  - Rebuilt environmental services scheduling around forecasted discharges instead of a flat shift, cutting clean-to-occupiable bed time from 71 to 38 minutes.
- **Result:** Average boarding time fell from 112 to 61 minutes, ED length of stay dropped 34 minutes, and we avoided opening a 12-bed surge unit that would have cost about $850K a year — all while holding occupancy at a safer 85%.
**Metrics to name:** ED boarding time; discharge-before-noon rate; clean-to-occupiable bed turnaround; occupancy and staffed-bed utilization; ED length of stay; admission-to-bed time.
**Trap to avoid:** Fixing the ED in isolation when the constraint is discharge timing and bed turnaround — that just moves the queue rather than clearing it.

### Q9. Our HCAHPS Communication with Nurses score is in the 20th percentile. How do you move it?
**Why they ask it:** Patient experience is scored, benchmarked, and reimbursed, and the answer shows whether you can operationalize culture change rather than just urge niceness.
**What strong candidates do:** Anchor to the national percentile, connect the fix to leader and hourly rounding, and tie behaviors to a specific standard.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** As patient experience director at a 300-bed hospital, I owned HCAHPS results where Communication with Nurses top-box was 74% — below the roughly 80% national median and in the low percentiles — with the biggest gap on "nurse explained things in a way I could understand."
- **Task:** I was tasked with moving the composite into the top quartile within four quarters, wired to clinical workflow rather than pure scripting.
- **Action:**
  - Drilled into unit-level data to find the gap was concentrated on nights and on two medical-surgical units, so we targeted there instead of hospital-wide.
  - Hardwired hourly rounding with a defined four-P script — pain, potty, position, possessions — plus daily nurse leader rounding on every patient, and used AIDET at every bedside introduction.
  - Gave every unit its own monthly HCAHPS dashboard, reviewed top-box trends at the leadership huddle, and required discharge teach-back so patients could verbalize their plan before leaving.
- **Result:** Communication with Nurses top-box rose from 74% to 84% over four quarters, moving us above the 75th percentile, and overall hospital rating rose from 68% to 77% — protecting an estimated $210K in annual Value-Based Purchasing incentive.
**Metrics to name:** HCAHPS Communication with Nurses top-box (median about 80%, 75th percentile higher); individual items (nurse courtesy about 86%, nurse listen 77%, nurse explain 75%); Overall Rating of Hospital; percentile ranking; Hospital VBP incentive.
**Trap to avoid:** Running a hospital-wide "smile more" campaign instead of finding the units, shifts, and specific questions where the gap actually lives.

### Q10. Our discharge information and care-transition scores are dragging our HCAHPS overall rating. What would you do?
**Why they ask it:** Discharge scores link directly to readmissions and reimbursement, so it tests whether you see experience and outcomes as connected.
**What strong candidates do:** Connect HCAHPS to transitions-of-care work and focus on early education and teach-back rather than paperwork at the door.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** At a 250-bed community hospital, our discharge information top-box score was 79% and communication about medicines was 55% — both below the national averages — and patients consistently told us they didn't know who to call or which medications to take.
- **Task:** As director of quality and patient experience, I owned the discharge experience metrics and was asked to bring both domains to the national average or better within two quarters.
- **Action:**
  - Audited 100 recent discharges and found the failure was timing — instructions were often delivered minutes before discharge, with no teach-back and no medication reconciliation before the ride arrived.
  - Moved to a "discharge starts at admission" model where nurses delivered education the day before, pharmacy reconciled medications at the bedside, and we required teach-back on the top three medications before discharge.
  - Added a nurse navigator call within 48 hours and a written after-visit summary with a direct clinic phone number, tracked as a unit metric.
- **Result:** Discharge information top-box rose from 79% to 87% and communication about medicines from 55% to 71% in two quarters, and the same transition-of-care work contributed to a 3.1-point reduction in our 30-day readmission rate.
**Metrics to name:** HCAHPS discharge information (national about 85%) and communication about medicines top-box; 48-hour follow-up call completion; medication reconciliation at discharge; teach-back completion; 30-day readmission rate.
**Trap to avoid:** Treating discharge as a paperwork task done at the door — experience and readmissions both improve only when education happens early with teach-back.

### Q11. You're running a 9% negative budget variance driven by overtime and unbudgeted nursing coverage. How do you get back to baseline without compromising patient safety?
**Why they ask it:** Budget stewardship is a core manager competency, and the question tests whether you can cut cost without harming quality.
**What strong candidates do:** Decompose the variance into volume, rate, and mix, protect patient safety, and present a sequenced plan with a specific recovery target.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** I was finance and operations manager for a 30-bed medical-surgical unit running a 9% unfavorable variance — about $410K year to date — driven by a 6.4% overtime rate and daily PRN coverage against a benchmark of premium labor under 2.5%.
- **Task:** I was accountable for the unit's labor budget and was told to recover the variance within two quarters without triggering patient safety or turnover problems.
- **Action:**
  - Decomposed the variance into volume, rate, and skill-mix components and found 68% was rate and premium labor, not volume — so this was a staffing-model problem, not a census problem.
  - Rebuilt the staffing grid against actual census patterns by shift, cross-trained four medical-surgical nurses to float to telemetry, and used a voluntary low-census flex program rather than mandating shifts.
  - Attacked controllable non-labor cost too — standardized supply par levels, cut waste on a top-20 supply SKU list, and renegotiated two service contracts, worth about $70K.
- **Result:** By quarter's end the unit's variance narrowed from −9% to −1.8%, overtime fell from 6.4% to 2.1%, and premium labor hours dropped 41% while nurse turnover held flat and HCAHPS nurse communication actually improved three points.
**Metrics to name:** Budget variance (target within plus or minus 2%); overtime as a percent of productive hours (ceiling 2–2.5%); agency and travel labor as a percent of labor expense (3–8%); labor cost per FTE and cost per adjusted admission; FTE per adjusted occupied bed (1st quartile about 3.4–3.5); worked hours per patient day; supply cost per case.
**Trap to avoid:** Leading with headcount cuts or a hiring freeze — the data usually shows premium-labor and staffing-model problems you can fix before touching the core team.

### Q12. One of our surgical service lines has a negative margin even though volume is growing. How do you find the cause?
**Why they ask it:** It tests whether you understand cost per case, contribution margin, and service-line economics rather than just top-line volume.
**What strong candidates do:** Break the line into cost, payer mix, and case mix, and distinguish a volume problem from a rate or cost problem.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Total joint replacement volume grew 14% year over year, but the service line's contribution margin fell from +$1.4M to −$310K, cost per case had climbed from $13,200 to $15,800, and the payer mix shifted six points toward Medicare Advantage.
- **Task:** As service line director, I was asked by the CFO to explain the margin loss and return the line to positive contribution within a year.
- **Action:**
  - Stratified margin by payer and DRG and found the loss concentrated in Medicare Advantage cases reimbursed below cost and in a 22% rise in implant cost per case.
  - Renegotiated implant and supply contracts and stood up a physician-led value analysis committee that standardized to two implant vendors, cutting implant cost $1,100 per case.
  - Reduced length of stay for joint replacements from 2.4 to 1.7 days with an enhanced recovery protocol and a same-day discharge pathway for low-risk patients, cutting variable cost per case.
- **Result:** Cost per case fell from $15,800 to $13,900, contribution margin recovered from −$310K to +$960K within three quarters, and LOS dropped 0.7 days with no increase in 30-day readmissions or ED returns.
**Metrics to name:** Cost per case and cost per discharge; contribution margin by service line; case mix index (community hospital typically about 1.3–1.6); payer mix; implant and supply cost per case; length of stay; 30-day readmission rate.
**Trap to avoid:** Chasing more volume to fix a margin problem — if the line loses money per case, growing volume just deepens the loss.

### Q13. Your service line spent $620K over budget on travel nurses last quarter. How do you stabilize it?
**Why they ask it:** Agency labor is one of the largest controllable cost drivers in hospitals, and the question tests retention-plus-recruitment thinking.
**What strong candidates do:** Quantify the premium, address the root cause in vacancy and retention, and set a glide path rather than a hard cutoff that leaves shifts uncovered.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** I managed a 46-bed telemetry and ICU service line where agency and travel nurse spend had reached 24% of nursing labor expense — far above the 3-to-8% benchmark for a well-managed hospital — and was $620K over budget for the quarter, with vacancy running at 18%.
- **Task:** I was accountable for labor cost and quality on the line and had to cut agency spend by half within two quarters without dropping below safe staffing ratios.
- **Action:**
  - Analyzed the vacancy pattern and found nine of fourteen open positions were on nights, so we were paying agency premiums for a scheduling-attractiveness problem, not an absolute shortage.
  - Created a night-shift differential plus a 12-week internal travel and float pool that paid a premium but 40% less than agency, and ran an internal referral program that filled six of the nine night roles.
  - Cut time-to-fill by redesigning hiring into a 21-day cycle and giving nurse managers interview authority, then layered in local contract labor as a lower-cost bridge for the remaining gaps.
- **Result:** Agency labor fell from 24% to 9% of nursing labor expense in five months, saving about $1.3M annualized; night-shift vacancy dropped from 18% to 6%; and nurse-sensitive indicators including falls and CAUTI held at or below their prior levels.
**Metrics to name:** Agency and travel labor as a percent of labor expense (benchmark 3–8%); vacancy and turnover rate; time-to-fill; labor cost per FTE; worked hours per patient day; nurse-sensitive indicators (falls, CAUTI, CLABSI).
**Trap to avoid:** Cutting agency spend on paper without fixing the vacancy and scheduling drivers — those shifts get picked up by overtime or go uncovered, and safety suffers.

### Q14. We're paying locum tenens rates to cover a specialty clinic and it's eating our margin. How do you reduce that dependence?
**Why they ask it:** Locum spend signals a recruitment or coverage-model problem, and the question tests whether you can build a sustainable plan.
**What strong candidates do:** Quantify the per-day premium, weigh permanent recruitment against an advanced practice provider model, and sequence interim coverage so access doesn't collapse.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** A hospital-owned orthopedics and general surgery clinic was spending $1.4M a year on locum tenens — roughly $1,500 to $1,800 per day plus travel — to cover two vacant surgeon positions, and the locums couldn't build a panel or take reliable call, so access suffered and referral leakage grew.
- **Task:** As clinic operations director, I was tasked with cutting locum spend by at least 40% within two years while stabilizing surgical access.
- **Action:**
  - Quantified the true all-in cost of locum coverage against recruitment and found a permanent surgeon broke even in about eleven months once I valued panel-building and call coverage, not just the daily rate.
  - Built a two-track coverage model — an orthopedics physician assistant and a general surgery advanced practice provider handling clinic visits and low-acuity procedures independently, plus a shared call schedule with a two-hospital partner.
  - Retained a physician recruiter with clear compensation bands and a faster credentialing pathway, using locum coverage only for irreducible gaps while we recruited.
- **Result:** Locum spend fell from $1.4M to $540K annually, two permanent surgeons were recruited within 14 months, and access improved — new-patient wait dropped from 47 to 18 days and referral leakage fell 22%.
**Metrics to name:** Locum and contract physician spend and per-day premium; recruitment cost and time-to-fill; advanced practice provider coverage ratio; third-next-available appointment; referral leakage; contribution margin per provider; wRVU production.
**Trap to avoid:** Eliminating locum coverage before permanent or advanced practice capacity is in place — you collapse the access and referral base you'll need to recruit into.

### Q15. Our OR prime-time utilization is 61% while block time is allocated by history. How do you improve it?
**Why they ask it:** The OR is one of the highest-cost, highest-revenue assets in the hospital, and utilization is the core metric of surgical operations.
**What strong candidates do:** Distinguish overall utilization from block utilization, benchmark to the 75-to-80% target, and use block release plus data-driven allocation.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** In a 10-room OR suite, prime-time utilization was 61% against a 75-to-80% benchmark, and surgeon block utilization ranged from 38% to 96%. Block time hadn't been reallocated in four years.
- **Task:** As perioperative services director, I owned OR throughput and was tasked with raising prime-time utilization above 75% within two quarters without damaging surgeon relationships.
- **Action:**
  - Built a block utilization report by surgeon and service and shared it one-on-one before any reallocation, so the conversation was about data rather than opinion.
  - Ran an eight-week trial with block release at 14 days and mandatory release at seven days, plus automated release rules so unused block returned to a pool open to all surgeons.
  - Increased add-on capacity by extending one room to 7pm on Tuesdays and Thursdays, staffing a 24-hour advanced practice coverage model, and moving high-variance cases earlier in the day to protect first-case starts.
- **Result:** Prime-time utilization rose from 61% to 78% in six months, and adding cases against the same fixed OR cost grew contribution margin by roughly $2.1M annualized; block utilization spread narrowed from 38–96% to 66–94%.
**Metrics to name:** Prime-time OR utilization (benchmark 75–80%); block utilization (typically a 65–70% minimum threshold); first-case on-time starts (90% target); turnover time (about 20 minutes); contribution margin per OR hour; add-on case volume.
**Trap to avoid:** Reallocating block time without a transparent data conversation with surgeons — clinical leadership will fight a decision that feels arbitrary, and you lose the change at the next renewal.

### Q16. First-case on-time starts are at 54% and turnover averages 38 minutes. What's your fix?
**Why they ask it:** These are the two most actionable OR efficiency levers, and the question tests whether you can run a tight perioperative process.
**What strong candidates do:** Diagnose the specific delay reasons, standardize the work, and set a numeric target tied to recovered capacity.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** For an eight-room OR suite, first-case on-time starts sat at 54% against the 90% benchmark and room turnover averaged 38 minutes against a roughly 20-minute benchmark. On a typical six-case day, the turnover gap alone was losing more than 90 minutes per room.
- **Task:** As OR manager, I owned the perioperative efficiency metrics and was asked to recover OR capacity without adding rooms or staff.
- **Action:**
  - Logged delay reasons for 200 cases and found 61% of first-case delays traced to pre-op readiness — missing history and physical, consent, or anesthesia clearance — not to the OR itself.
  - Created a pre-op readiness checklist completed the day before surgery and owned by the scheduling and pre-assessment teams, moved the pre-op huddle to 6:45am, and gave anesthesia a 24-hour case review so disagreements surfaced before the morning.
  - Standardized turnover with a parallel-process playbook and dedicated turnover team, used a visual timer in each room, and posted start-to-incision and turnover time on a public OR board.
