# 30 Revenue Cycle & Patient Access Interview Questions with STAR Answer Scripts

> **How to use this section:** These are example scripts. Adapt the numbers, systems, and payer names to your own experience — an interview answer that sounds like someone else's job is worse than a shorter, true one. Roles covered: Revenue Cycle Manager, Revenue Integrity Analyst, Patient Access Manager, Patient Financial Services / Financial Counselor, Denial Management Specialist, Pre-Authorization / Utilization Review Coordinator, Billing and Coding Manager, and Patient Access Representative progressing to Supervisor.

---

### Q1. Walk me through a time you reduced your initial denial rate. (Revenue Cycle Manager)
**Why they ask it:** Denials are the single largest controllable revenue leak in the cycle, and they want a methodology, not a war story.
**What strong candidates do:** Name the baseline denial rate, segment it by root cause (registration, auth, medical necessity, coding), attack the top three drivers, and quantify the dollars recovered.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** At a 240-bed community hospital, our initial denial rate had climbed to 11.4% over two quarters after a payer tightened medical-necessity review on observation accounts. Denials represented about $3.1M in annualized billed charges, and our appeal overturn rate was only 38%.
- **Task:** I was accountable for the denial reduction initiative and for the appeal workflow, reporting monthly to the CFO.
- **Action:**
  - I pulled 12 months of 835 remittance data and stratified denials by CARC code, payer, and service line, which showed that CO-50 (medical necessity) and CO-197 (no prior authorization) were 62% of the total.
  - I built a weekly denial prevention huddle with utilization review and the physician advisor to challenge observation-versus-inpatient decisions before the bill dropped, using InterQual criteria as the screening standard.
  - I stood up a two-tier appeal process — clinical appeals drafted by an RN with peer-to-peer escalation for medical necessity, and technical appeals worked within the 180-day filing windows — with a 15-day internal turnaround standard.
- **Result:** Initial denial rate fell from 11.4% to 6.8% in nine months, appeal overturn rate rose to 71%, and we recovered $1.9M in previously written-off charges.
**Metrics to name:** Initial denial rate, denial write-off dollars, appeal overturn rate, denial rate by CARC code, net collection rate.
**Trap to avoid:** Claiming you "fought every denial" — payers win some, and a credible answer shows you triage by dollar value and win probability.

### Q2. We have a 90+ day A/R bucket that keeps growing. How have you fixed that? (Revenue Cycle Manager)
**Why they ask it:** Aging A/R is the clearest test of whether you manage work queues or just watch reports.
**What strong candidates do:** Segment the aging by payer and root cause, rebuild patient-level work queues by dollar value, and set daily touch standards rather than monthly cleanups.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** A multi-specialty physician group had 41% of its $8.6M A/R sitting in the 90+ bucket, with days in A/R at 62 and an unbilled DNFB backlog of 1,100 encounters.
- **Task:** I was brought in to reduce the aging, with a target of getting days in A/R under 40 within two quarters.
- **Action:**
  - I ran an A/R inventory by aging bucket and payer, and found $2.3M was in three fixable categories: no-response claims older than 60 days, coordination-of-benefits denials, and true self-pay.
  - I reorganized the work queues so staff worked highest-balance claims first by payer-behavior tier, and I set a floor of 35 claims touched per FTE per day with daily productivity reporting.
  - I implemented 276/277 claim status transactions to replace manual portal checks, which freed about 90 staff hours a week, and pushed unresolved claims older than 120 days into a defined write-off or agency-transfer policy.
- **Result:** Days in A/R dropped from 62 to 38 in five months, the 90+ bucket fell from 41% to 16% of total A/R, and cash collections rose $640K in the first full quarter.
**Metrics to name:** Days in A/R, A/R greater than 90 days as a percentage of total, DNFB days, touches per FTE per day, write-off percentage.
**Trap to avoid:** Saying you "cleaned up the aging" without addressing why claims aged in the first place — a one-time cleanup that regresses is a red flag.

### Q3. What have you done to improve your clean claim rate from the front end? (Patient Access Manager)
**Why they ask it:** Most denials are preventable at registration, and they want to know if you own upstream data quality.
**What strong candidates do:** Tie registration data quality to downstream denial rates, measure front-end error sources, and build edit and feedback loops with the back end.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Our clean claim rate was 88% against a system target of 96%, and a sample of 200 denied claims showed 47% of the root causes traced to registration — eligibility not verified, wrong subscriber data, and missing authorization numbers.
- **Task:** As Patient Access Manager I owned preregistration, verification, and the accuracy of the demographics and insurance data feeding the 837 claim file.
- **Action:**
  - I instituted a 100% preregistration standard at least two business days before service, with real-time 270/271 eligibility checks so staff worked exceptions instead of guessing at coverage.
  - I built a front-end denial cause-and-effect report that traced each denied claim back to the specific registrar and edit, reviewed monthly so feedback was about the edit, not the person.
  - I created a hard-stop in the registration workflow that blocked account finalization when the authorization field or PCP referral was blank for the affected plans.
- **Result:** Clean claim rate rose from 88% to 96.3% in two quarters, front-end-related denials dropped 58%, and registration accuracy auditing moved from 91% to 98.6% on the fields tied to claim edits.
**Metrics to name:** Clean claim rate, registration accuracy audit score, eligibility verification rate, authorization attached rate, front-end denial rate by cause code.
**Trap to avoid:** Pushing everything back on billing — if your answer has no front-end edit or verification step, you have not addressed the real problem.

### Q4. How do you drive point-of-service collections without hurting the patient experience? (Patient Access Manager)
**Why they ask it:** POS collections are pure margin improvement, but they require scripting, technology, and staff coaching that many managers never build.
**What strong candidates do:** Use pre-service estimates and financial clearance, script the ask, and measure collections as a percentage of point-of-service liability rather than a lump dollar figure.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Our POS collections represented only 2.1% of total patient responsibility, and staff told me they avoided asking for money because it felt aggressive in an emergency department lobby.
- **Task:** I was asked to grow point-of-service collections while keeping patient satisfaction flat or better.
- **Action:**
  - I worked with IT to turn on real-time benefit checks and a good-faith-estimate-style pre-service estimate at scheduling, so the copay or deductible figure was correct before the patient arrived.
  - I trained staff on a scripted, non-apologetic ask — "Your estimated responsibility today is $150" — and gave them tools: card on file, text-to-pay, and a documented payment plan option down to a defined threshold, so "no" was never the only alternative.
  - I set a daily POS dashboard by registrar and department, and paired the bottom three performers with the top performer for peer coaching rather than disciplinary action.
- **Result:** POS collections grew from 2.1% to 7.8% of patient responsibility in six months, adding roughly $410K in cash, while the ED patient experience score held steady at the 78th percentile.
**Metrics to name:** POS collections as a percentage of patient responsibility, upfront cash collected, point-of-service collection dollars per registrar, copay collection rate.
**Trap to avoid:** Describing collections as a hard sell — a credible answer includes charity screening and payment plans, because asking the wrong patient for the wrong amount is what destroys satisfaction.

### Q5. Tell me about a payer contract dispute you managed. (Revenue Cycle Manager / Contracting)
**Why they ask it:** They want to see whether you understand contracted reimbursement mechanics or just escalate emotionally.
**What strong candidates do:** Quantify the variance against contracted terms, build the evidence package from remittance data, and escalate through defined channels with a dollar ask.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** A regional commercial payer that was 19% of our payer mix began reimbursing outpatient surgery at what looked like a per-diem rate instead of the contracted case rate, and six months of underpayments totaled roughly $780K.
- **Task:** I owned the underpayment analysis and the provider-relations escalation on this payer.
- **Action:**
  - I exported the 835 remittances and compared allowed amounts against our loaded contract fee schedule and case rates, producing a line-level variance report showing a 22% shortfall on 340 claims.
  - I assembled the evidence package — contract language, claim-level detail, and the calculated expected-versus-actual reimbursement — and submitted it through the payer's dispute portal while simultaneously raising it with our provider relations representative.
  - I escalated to the joint operating committee when the portal produced no response in 30 days, and negotiated a retroactive reprocessing agreement plus a 90-day monitoring report so we could catch recurrence.
- **Result:** The payer reprocessed $712K within two quarters, we recovered 91% of the identified variance, and the monitoring report cut repeat underpayments from 340 claims to fewer than 30 in the following period.
**Metrics to name:** Underpayment variance dollars, expected versus actual reimbursement, contract variance percentage, percentage of Medicare, payer mix, recovery rate on identified variance.
**Trap to avoid:** Calling it a fight with the payer — the winning answer is analytical, contract-driven, and escalates on evidence, not volume.

### Q6. Explain how you would approach a chargemaster pricing correction. (Revenue Integrity Analyst)
**Why they ask it:** The CDM drives both charge capture and cash, and they want to know if you understand that pricing and coding are one system.
**What strong candidates do:** Reference charge description master maintenance, code and revenue code accuracy, payer-specific pricing, and the operational risk of getting it wrong mid-year.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** During a charge capture review I found that 14 pharmacy and supply charges on our CDM had not been updated in over two years, and several revenue codes did not match the HCPCS descriptors, creating both lost charges and audit exposure.
- **Task:** As Revenue Integrity Analyst I was responsible for recommending CDM corrections with documentation and change control.
- **Action:**
  - I rebuilt the affected charge records using current HCPCS and CPT code sets, corrected revenue codes, and applied our cost-based markup methodology so charges stayed defensible under price transparency requirements.
  - I ran an impact model against six months of utilization to estimate gross and net revenue effects, and cleared the changes through the revenue integrity committee with clinical and compliance sign-off before activation.
  - I implemented a quarterly CDM review calendar and a change log so pricing changes were version-controlled rather than made ad hoc in the system.
- **Result:** The corrections recovered about $290K in previously missed annualized charges, eliminated 14 revenue-code mismatches flagged in the internal audit, and quarterly CDM reviews have caught 22 additional charge-descriptor issues since.
**Metrics to name:** Charge capture variance, gross-to-net revenue impact, CDM maintenance cycle, revenue code accuracy, charge lag days.
**Trap to avoid:** Treating pricing as a finance-only exercise — uncoordinated CDM edits break contract modeling, decision support, and price transparency all at once.

### Q7. How have you fixed a prior authorization bottleneck? (Pre-Authorization / Utilization Review Coordinator)
**Why they ask it:** Auth failures are the top preventable denial category, and the answer reveals whether you manage workflow or just work hard.
**What strong candidates do:** Move auth earlier in the schedule, identify high-volume auth-required codes, and standardize submission packets to reduce rework.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Our prior authorization turnaround averaged 5.8 days on imaging and surgical cases, and 18% of authorization denials were caused by either late submission or the service proceeding before the auth number was loaded to the account.
- **Task:** As the Pre-Authorization Coordinator I owned submission, tracking, and handoff to the clinical and billing teams.
- **Action:**
  - I built a payer-specific auth matrix mapping the CPT and HCPCS codes that required authorization by plan, so schedulers could flag auth-required cases at the point of scheduling rather than the day before service.
  - I standardized the clinical packet — diagnosis, medical necessity documentation, and prior conservative treatment — using InterQual and MCG criteria screens so first-pass approvals rose and we stopped resubmitting incomplete requests.
  - I created a daily pending-auth report with escalation rules at 48 hours to the payer's utilization management line and at 72 hours to our physician advisor for peer-to-peer, plus a hard stop verifying the auth number was attached before the encounter could finalize.
- **Result:** Average auth turnaround dropped from 5.8 days to 2.1 days, auth-related denials fell 74%, and we eliminated services performed without authorization entirely within one quarter.
**Metrics to name:** Prior authorization turnaround days, first-pass auth approval rate, auth-related denial rate, percentage of services performed without authorization.
**Trap to avoid:** Moaning about payer requirements — the interviewer wants your process controls, not your opinion of the payer.

### Q8. You get a coding audit finding that shows systematic upcoding. What do you do? (Billing & Coding Manager)
**Why they ask it:** This is a compliance and integrity question disguised as a technical one, and the answer has to show you can hold a hard line with clinicians.
**What strong candidates do:** Pull the sample, quantify the financial exposure, use certified auditors, distinguish documentation gaps from intentional error, and correct forward while addressing the past.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** An external audit of 60 evaluation and management encounters found a 21% overcode rate against 2021 E/M guidelines, largely because clinicians were selecting level 4 visits on complexity without documenting the medical decision-making to support it. Estimated repayment exposure was $140K across a two-year lookback.
- **Task:** As Billing and Coding Manager I had to remediate the findings, correct the process, and present a defensible plan to compliance.
- **Action:**
  - I engaged our certified professional coders to re-audit an expanded stratified sample by provider and specialty, so we could distinguish isolated documentation failures from a systemic pattern.
  - I built targeted provider education around medical decision-making elements and time-based coding, and delivered it provider by provider with their own de-identified charts rather than a generic slide deck.
  - I implemented a pre-bill coding review for the highest-risk provider-specialty combinations and a quarterly internal audit cycle, with findings reported to the compliance committee and repayments handled through the standard self-disclosure route where warranted.
- **Result:** Re-audit six months later showed the overcode rate down to 4.2%, no further repayment exposure in the review period, and the internal audit program has since been expanded to outpatient surgery at no additional FTE.
**Metrics to name:** Coding audit accuracy rate, overcode/undercode rate, medical necessity denial rate, documentation improvement rate, repayment exposure.
**Trap to avoid:** Saying you would "educate and move on" — systematic upcoding is a False Claims Act risk, and any credible answer includes disclosure and monitoring.

### Q9. How do you decide what to write off versus what to pursue? (Patient Financial Services Manager)
**Why they ask it:** Denial write-offs are the quiet leak nobody tracks, and the question tests whether you manage by policy or by convenience.
**What strong candidates do:** Work by dollar threshold and appeal economics, track write-off reasons in categories, and get reimbursement recovered before the account ages out.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Our denial write-offs were running $1.4M a year, and when I pulled the accounts I found 30% were written off inside 45 days — before the payer's own appeal window had even closed.
- **Task:** I owned the denial work queue and the write-off approval policy for a 12-provider group.
- **Action:**
  - I created a write-off reason taxonomy — medical necessity, no authorization, timely filing, non-covered, coding — so we could see patterns rather than a single lump number.
  - I set a policy that anything above $100 required documented appeal at least through the first level, and I built a tiered approval chain so write-offs above $500 needed my sign-off with a rationale.
  - I redirected the work queue so staff worked denials by expected recovery value, not by date, and I built payer-specific playbooks for our three highest-denial payers with the exact appeal language and documentation they consistently accepted.
- **Result:** Denial write-offs fell 43% year over year, from $1.4M to $800K, while our average denial resolution time shortened from 52 to 31 days.
**Metrics to name:** Denial write-off dollars and percentage, write-off by reason category, appeal rate on denials above threshold, dollars recovered per denial FTE.
**Trap to avoid:** Presenting write-offs as unavoidable — interviewers listen for whether you can tell the difference between an uncollectible account and an unworked one.

### Q10. How would you improve insurance verification and eligibility? (Patient Access Manager)
**Why they ask it:** Eligibility errors create denials, bad debt, and patient complaints, and the fix is a workflow problem, not a software problem.
**What strong candidates do:** Use real-time eligibility transactions, push verification to preregistration, define what "verified" means, and handle the exceptions explicitly.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Our eligibility-related denials were 9% of all denials, and a spot audit showed staff were verifying coverage by reading the card rather than running an eligibility check — so terminated coverage and COB issues only surfaced when the claim denied.
- **Task:** I owned preregistration and verification for two hospital-based clinics.
- **Action:**
  - I moved verification to 100% preregistration and mandated a real-time 270/271 check with the response printed to the account, defining "verified" as an active coverage response, not a card image.
  - I built exception queues for secondary coverage, COB, and Medicare Advantage plans, because those were where staff were most likely to skip the check, and added a daily eligibility worklist of accounts with no response.
  - I partnered with scheduling to capture and confirm insurance at the time the appointment is booked, and I added a monthly re-verification sweep for recurring infusion and therapy patients whose plans change frequently.
- **Result:** Eligibility denials dropped from 9% to 2.6% of total denials, uninsured accounts at registration fell 31%, and preregistration verification compliance rose to 98% in three months.
**Metrics to name:** Eligibility denial rate, eligibility verification compliance percentage, uninsured/self-pay percentage at registration, COB identification rate, preregistration completion rate.
**Trap to avoid:** Relying on "the software does it" — every system lets exceptions through and someone has to own the worklist.

### Q11. Tell me about a difficult conversation with a patient about the cost of care. (Financial Counselor)
**Why they ask it:** Financial counseling is where the organization's integrity and its cash collide, and they want empathy plus policy command.
**What strong candidates do:** Open with a clear estimate, screen for financial assistance proactively, present real options, and document the conversation and the patient's choice.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** An uninsured patient came to me with a $14,200 estimate for an elective orthopedic procedure, visibly distressed and ready to cancel the surgery because he assumed payment in full was required before service.
- **Task:** As the Financial Counselor I had to present the obligation honestly while identifying every program we could legitimately apply.
- **Action:**
  - I walked through the written estimate line by line, then screened him for Medicaid, our financial assistance policy under 501(r), and the hospital's sliding fee scale — he landed at a discount tier that reduced the balance to about $3,100.
  - I presented concrete options: an interest-free payment plan within our policy limits, a prompt-pay discount, and a good-faith estimate documentation requirement under the No Surprises Act so he knew nothing would be billed beyond the quote.
  - I documented the screening, the discount determination, and the payment plan he selected, and I scheduled a follow-up call so he was not left to navigate the billing statements alone.
- **Result:** He kept the procedure on the schedule, enrolled on a 24-month payment plan with a 92% on-time payment history, and my documentation was used as the model for our uninsured patient counseling workflow.
**Metrics to name:** Charity care and financial assistance enrollment rate, self-pay collection rate, bad debt as a percentage of self-pay, point-of-service cash, payment plan adherence.
**Trap to avoid:** Framing it as either collections or compassion — the strongest answers show both, because patients who understand the options pay more reliably.

### Q12. What does price transparency compliance require, and how did you handle it? (Revenue Cycle Manager)
**Why they ask it:** The Hospital Price Transparency Rule and No Surprises Act are live compliance obligations, and many candidates have never touched them.
**What strong candidates do:** Reference the machine-readable file and shoppable services requirements, assign ownership, and treat the file as the same data that drives estimates.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Our hospital had a machine-readable standard charge file posted, but it was failing the CMS format checks on several required fields, and we had no documented process for keeping it current after chargemaster changes.
- **Task:** I took ownership of price transparency compliance for the revenue cycle, in partnership with compliance and IT.
- **Action:**
  - I audited the file against the required data elements — gross charge, discounted cash price, and the minimum and maximum negotiated rates by payer — and corrected the missing elements using our contract management system rather than manual entry.
  - I built a patient-facing shoppable services estimator covering our planned services and tied it to the same CDM data, so estimates and the published file could not drift apart.
  - I established a scheduled refresh tied to each chargemaster and contract update, with a compliance sign-off step and a change log, and I documented the process so it survived personnel changes.
- **Result:** The file passed internal compliance validation on the next cycle, our shoppable services estimator now covers all 300 planned services, and we closed the exposure to potential CMS penalties while reducing pricing calls to the business office.
**Metrics to name:** Price transparency file compliance rate, shoppable services coverage, estimate accuracy, CDM change cycle, No Surprises Act good-faith estimate compliance.
**Trap to avoid:** Treating it as a one-time IT project — the rule requires the file to reflect current data continuously, so the process matters more than the initial upload.

### Q13. How do you improve productivity in a billing team without burning people out? (Billing & Coding Manager)
**Why they ask it:** They want to know whether you can raise throughput through process and measurement rather than pressure.
**What strong candidates do:** Measure the right unit of work, remove the low-value tasks through automation, and pair productivity data with quality so nobody games the numbers.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Our billing team of nine FTEs was processing about 4,800 claims a month, with an average of 22 touches per FTE per day, and late charges and rework were eating the gains.
- **Task:** I was responsible for the department's productivity and accuracy as Billing and Coding Manager.
- **Action:**
  - I established a productivity standard by claim type — professional, facility, and high-complexity — instead of one blunt number, so we were comparing like work to like work and staff accepted the target.
  - I automated the routine work: clearinghouse edit scrubbing before submission, eligibility checks, and electronic remittance posting, which removed about 30 hours a week of manual posting and portal checking.
  - I paired the productivity dashboard with a quality metric — first-pass acceptance rate and rework percentage — and used weekly 15-minute team huddles to solve blockers together rather than publish individual rankings.
- **Result:** Throughput rose from 22 to 34 clean claims per FTE per day with the same headcount, first-pass acceptance improved from 91% to 96%, and the department's voluntary turnover was zero over the following year.
**Metrics to name:** Claims per FTE per day, first-pass acceptance rate, rework percentage, charge lag days, cost to collect.
**Trap to avoid:** Talking only about speed — a productivity answer without a quality counterweight tells the interviewer you would trade accuracy for volume.

### Q14. Tell me about a time you transitioned to a new EHR or billing system. (Revenue Cycle Manager)
**Why they ask it:** Systems conversions are where revenue cycle teams either shine or lose millions, and they want someone who planned the operational side.
**What strong candidates do:** Talk about work queue and rule rebuild, payer enrollment and clearinghouse setup, parallel-run testing, and protecting cash during the cutover.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Our organization migrated from a legacy billing platform to Epic for a 30-provider group, and the risk was that AR would stall during the freeze while the transition rules, edits, and payer connections were rebuilt.
- **Task:** I led revenue cycle readiness on the project, covering registration, charge capture, claims, and remittance posting.
- **Action:**
  - I mapped every historical work queue, claim edit, and billing rule to its new-system equivalent and rebuilt payer-specific rules for our top eight payers, testing each with sample claim files before go-live.
  - I managed the clearinghouse transition and payer enrollment cutover in advance, including EDI enrollment for 837 claims and 835 remittance, so claims could flow on day one rather than after a gap.
  - I planned a two-week pre-go-live push to reduce the old system AR below 90 days and trained staff on the new work queues with a super-user on each pod, then scheduled daily cash reconciliation during the first 60 days.
- **Result:** We held days in A/R flat through the transition at 36 days, first-pass claim acceptance was 94% in the first month post-go-live, and we avoided the denial spike that typically follows a conversion by tracking edits daily for eight weeks.
**Metrics to name:** Days in A/R through transition, first-pass claim acceptance post-go-live, DNFB days, cash collections versus budget, edit/denial volume by week.
**Trap to avoid:** Describing the conversion as an IT project — interviewers know revenue cycle must own the design of the downstream workflow or the department fails.

### Q15. Walk me through a complex denial appeal you managed. (Denial Management Specialist)
**Why they ask it:** This is the core skill of the role and it reveals whether you understand appeal rights and levels or just resubmit.
**What strong candidates do:** Identify the denial type, use the right appeal level, gather supporting documentation, and know the filing deadlines and escalation path.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** A $68,000 inpatient surgical claim was denied for medical necessity with CO-50, and the payer stated the documentation did not support acute inpatient care versus observation. The claim was past the initial appeal window if I missed the 60-day filing deadline.
- **Task:** I owned the appeal as the Denial Management Specialist and had to build a clinically defensible case within 45 days.
- **Action:**
  - I requested the complete medical record, physician statement, and the specific clinical criteria the payer applied, which is a required step before a meaningful medical necessity appeal — you cannot rebut criteria you have not seen.
  - I worked with the utilization review nurse and the attending physician to draft a clinical appeal that mapped the documentation directly to InterQual inpatient criteria and included the physician's attestation and a peer-to-peer request.
  - I filed at the first level within the window and, when it was upheld, escalated to the second level with the corrective action plan path and engaged our provider relations representative while tracking the claim in the appeal status on the 276/277.
- **Result:** The payer overturned the denial at the second level and paid $68,000 in full, and I turned the criteria mapping into a template now used for all inpatient medical necessity appeals, which lifted our overturn rate from 42% to 69%.
**Metrics to name:** Appeal rate, appeal overturn rate, average days to appeal, dollars recovered, denial aging in the appeal queue.
**Trap to avoid:** Skipping the level structure — saying you "appealed" without naming the level, the deadline, or the documentation shows the interviewer you have never actually worked a denial queue.

### Q16. How do you handle an uninsured patient who cannot pay anything? (Financial Counselor)
**Why they ask it:** This is a compliance and mission question — they want to see whether you know the financial assistance policy or make it up as you go.
**What strong candidates do:** Screen for every eligibility pathway, apply the financial assistance policy and presumptive eligibility rules, and document the determination.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** A patient with no insurance presented with a $9,400 emergency department and inpatient balance, and our self-pay collection rate had been slipping because counselors were sending uninsured accounts to bad debt before screening them.
- **Task:** As Financial Counselor I owned financial clearance and counseling for self-pay and uninsured accounts.
- **Action:**
  - I ran the standard screening sequence — Medicaid eligibility, marketplace coverage options, our financial assistance policy, and presumptive eligibility based on existing data such as prior enrollment or a provider's homeless and low-income documentation.
  - Because the patient qualified under our sliding scale, I applied the discount determination, documented the screening and the policy applied in the account notes, and removed the account from the collections queue.
  - I escalated a process gap I had found — uninsured accounts entering collections without a screening timestamp — and proposed a hard stop so no self-pay account could be written off without a documented financial assistance determination.
- **Result:** The patient's balance reduced by roughly 85% under the policy, bad debt placement of uninsured accounts fell 46% within two quarters, and the screening hard stop brought our financial assistance documentation to 100% on audited accounts.
**Metrics to name:** Financial assistance screening rate, charity care dollars and percentage of net revenue, uninsured bad debt, self-pay collection rate, presumptive eligibility determinations.
**Trap to avoid:** Offering a payment plan to someone who qualifies for charity care — under 501(r) and basic ethics, screening comes first, and skipping it exposes the organization.

### Q17. How have you cross-trained a revenue cycle team? (Revenue Cycle Manager)
**Why they ask it:** Coverage risk and staff development are real management concerns, and they want to know if you build bench strength deliberately.
**What strong candidates do:** Identify single points of failure, build a skills matrix, rotate work, and measure readiness rather than counting training hours.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Our denial and cash posting work was concentrated in two experienced staff, and a single unplanned absence could stall the cash posting backlog by a week and push DNFB days past target.
- **Task:** I needed to build cross-coverage across denial management, cash posting, and credit resolution without degrading productivity.
- **Action:**
  - I built a skills matrix by function and FTE and identified 11 workflows with only one trained person, which gave us a prioritized cross-training plan rather than a general "everyone learn everything" push.
  - I paired each specialist with a backup and ran structured rotations on the high-risk queues, with a documented checklist and a competency sign-off before the backup was counted as covered.
  - I tied the training to career paths — patient access to financial counseling, denial specialist to revenue integrity — so people had a reason to learn beyond covering vacations.
- **Result:** Within four months every critical workflow had at least two trained staff, unplanned absence coverage went from 60% to 100%, DNFB days stayed at or under three through two absences that would previously have broken the queue, and two internal staff were promoted into specialist roles.
**Metrics to name:** Skills matrix coverage percentage, single-point-of-failure count, DNFB days, work queue backlog, internal promotion rate.
**Trap to avoid:** Describing cross-training as a one-time event — without a skills matrix and competency checks, it is just a meeting.

### Q18. What is a net collection rate, and how have you moved yours? (Revenue Cycle Manager)
**Why they ask it:** Net collection rate is the definitive measure of whether you are collecting what you are actually owed, allowing for contractual adjustments.
**What strong candidates do:** Define it correctly — payments divided by charges net of contractual allowances — and explain that you move it by removing denials, underpayments, and front-end leakage.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Our net collection rate had drifted to 91.2% against an expected range near 97%, meaning we were losing roughly 6% of collectible revenue to denials, underpayments, and unworked accounts.
- **Task:** I owned net collection rate as a primary KPI for the revenue cycle department.
- **Action:**
  - I broke the 91.2% into its components — initial denials, underpayments, timely filing failures, and self-pay leakage — so we attacked the largest drivers rather than the whole number.
  - I implemented a pre-submission scrubber to catch the top ten edit categories before claims left the system, dropping our denial rate, and I added a weekly underpayment review against contracted rates for our top five payers.
  - I tightened the front end by requiring eligibility verification and upfront collection at the point of service, and I built a payer scorecard so leadership tracked performance against expected reimbursement monthly.
- **Result:** Net collection rate improved from 91.2% to 97.4% over four quarters, contractual adjustment accuracy improved, and we recovered an estimated $1.1M in annualized collectible revenue.
**Metrics to name:** Net collection rate, initial denial rate, underpayment recovery dollars, contractual adjustment accuracy, gross collection rate, cost to collect.
**Trap to avoid:** Using net collection rate and gross collection rate interchangeably — mixing them signals you quote KPIs without understanding them.

### Q19. How do you reduce medical necessity denials specifically? (Denial Management Specialist)
**Why they ask it:** Medical necessity denials are the hardest category and the one where prevention matters more than appeals.
**What strong candidates do:** Reference clinical criteria such as InterQual or MCG, the physician advisor relationship, and documentation integrity before the bill drops.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** Medical necessity denials were our largest denial category at 34% of all denials and $2.1M in billed charges, with most arising from inpatient admissions that the payer classified as observation under the two-midnight standard.
- **Task:** I was responsible for both preventing and appealing this denial category.
- **Action:**
  - I worked with utilization review to screen every admission against InterQual and MCG criteria at admission and at 24 hours, with an escalation path to the physician advisor when the criteria screen was marginal.
  - I got the physician advisor and the attending physicians to document the clinical rationale in the progress notes at the time of the admission decision, since a strong peer-to-peer argument cannot be reconstructed a month later if the record does not reflect it.
  - For the denials filed anyway, I built a payer-specific appeal library with the criteria mapping and clinical rationales that each payer had historically accepted, and tracked first-level versus second-level overturn rates separately.
- **Result:** Medical necessity denials fell 51% year over year, our appeal overturn rate on the remaining denials climbed to 74%, and denied dollars recovered on this category alone were $940K in twelve months.
**Metrics to name:** Medical necessity denial rate, observation-to-inpatient conversion rate, appeal overturn rate by level, physician advisor escalation rate, denied dollars recovered.
**Trap to avoid:** Saying "we appeal everything" — if you appeal denials that lack clinical support you burn credibility with the payer and lose leverage on the cases that matter.

### Q21. What are days in A/R, and how would you bring ours down from 55 days? (Revenue Cycle Manager)
**Why they ask it:** It is the primary benchmark for revenue cycle efficiency and tests whether you understand what actually drives it.
**What strong candidates do:** Explain the calculation, note the hospital-versus-physician benchmark difference, and attack the drivers — DNFB, denials, payer turnaround, and unworked accounts.
**STAR Script:**
- **Situation:** A physician services department I supported was running 55 days in A/R against a best-practice target near 35 to 40 days, and I traced a large share of it to unbilled encounters sitting in the charge entry and coding queues.
- **Task:** I was tasked with reducing days in A/R while holding net collection rate steady, since it is easy to lower the number by writing off accounts.
- **Action:**
  - I measured what I called A/R velocity and found nearly 20 days of the total was in the pre-bill stage, so I attacked charge lag first by automating electronic charge capture and setting a 48-hour charge entry standard with a daily lag report.
  - I reduced the DNFB backlog — mainly encounters pending coding queries — by establishing a coding query turnaround target and a weekly reconciliation between the charge log and the scheduling system to catch missed charges.
  - I segmented the post-bill A/R by payer, moved the highest-risk accounts into earlier follow-up at 14 days instead of 30, and put a defined escalation path on claims that hit 60 days so nothing aged passively.
- **Result:** Days in A/R moved from 55 to 37 in six months, DNFB days dropped from 8.5 to 2.9, and the net collection rate improved slightly from 95.1% to 96.4%, confirming we had not reduced the number by writing off collectible accounts.
**Metrics to name:** Days in A/R, DNFB days, charge lag days, A/R over 90 days, net collection rate, clean claim rate.
**Trap to avoid:** Reporting a lower days in A/R without showing collections held or improved — a rising write-off rate alongside a falling A/R number is a failed answer.

### Q24. What coding edits or modifier errors most commonly cause denials, and how have you addressed them? (Billing & Coding Manager)

### Q30. Our revenue cycle team is short-staffed and morale is low. What do you do? (Revenue Cycle Manager)
