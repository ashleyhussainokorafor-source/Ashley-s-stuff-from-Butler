## SECTION 1 — What each degree actually is (0:30–2:30)

**The one-line version:**
- **MPA = Master of Public Administration.** You are trained to run *government and
  publicly-funded systems* — policy, budgets, public health departments, city and county
  health agencies, non-profits.
- **MHA = Master of Health Administration.** You are trained to run *healthcare
  organisations* — hospitals, clinics, physician groups, surgery centres, payers.

**Say this plainly:**
> "MPA is the degree for people who want to change how the system works. MHA is the degree
> for people who want to run the operation inside the system. Both are leadership degrees.
> They are aimed at different employers."

**On screen:** two columns, employer logos by type — MPA: county health department, CMS,
hospital association, policy shop. MHA: hospital, ambulatory group, ASC, health plan.

---

## SECTION 2 — Where the confusion actually comes from (2:30–4:00)

This is the section that earns the watch time, because it explains why they're confused.

> "Here's the trap. A lot of MPA programs have a **health concentration**, and a lot of MHA
> programs teach **policy.** So the curriculum overlaps and the brochures sound identical.
>
> The difference is not the coursework. It's **who recruits at your career fair.**"

**This is the whole video's thesis.** Say it twice.

- MPA recruiting: government agencies, public health departments, policy organisations, non-profits
- MHA recruiting: hospitals, health systems, physician groups, payers, consulting

> "Look at which employers come to campus. That tells you what the degree is *for* far more
> honestly than the course list."

---

## SECTION 3 — The decision question (4:00–5:30)

Give them the tool. This is what people screenshot and share.

> "Ask yourself: **do I want to fix the system, or run the operation?**
>
> - If your honest answer is *fix the system* — policy, access, public health, equity — **MPA.**
> - If your honest answer is *run the operation* — staffing, flow, margins, service lines — **MHA.**
>
> And if the answer is *'I just want to be a hospital CEO'* — then neither degree is the
> deciding factor. Which brings me to the part people don't want to hear."

---

## SECTION 4 — The honest part (5:30–7:00)

This is where you separate from every other video on the topic, and it directly addresses the
comment *"can a MBA in healthcare help a person become CEO?"*

> "No degree makes you a CEO. Not MPA, not MHA, not an MBA.
>
> CEOs come from **operating experience** — you've run a service line, you've owned a P&L,
> you've been accountable for a number. The degree gets you into the room earlier. It does
> not skip the room.
>
> So the question is not which degree. It's **which degree opens the first door you can walk
> through.**"

Then the practical ranking for a US hospital career:
1. **MHA** — most direct line into hospital and health-system operations
2. **MPA** — strongest for public health, government, policy, non-profit leadership
3. **MBA w/ healthcare concentration** — good, but you'll need to prove healthcare fluency
   separately; it does not signal clinical or operational literacy by itself

---

## SECTION 5 — The zero-experience problem (7:00–8:30)

Directly answers *"How can I enter with a master's and no experience?"* — the #2 question on
the channel (9 of 50).

> "Here's what I actually see. People finish the master's with **no operational experience**,
> then wonder why the door won't open.
>
> The degree is not the door. The door is the entry role. **Get the entry role before or
> during the degree, not after it.**"
>
> - Patient access / scheduling
> - Revenue cycle analyst (hires faster than most, and it teaches you the money)
> - Administrative fellowship (the real fast lane, if the system offers one)
> - Compliance / quality (chronically under-applied-for)

> "If you're choosing between two programs and one has an administrative fellowship and the
> other doesn't — that fellowship is worth more than the ranking difference."
